What makes an algorithm greedy?

Greedy commits to local choices. That is why it is fast (no search), and also why it can be wrong: an early choice can rule out a better answer later. Many greedy algorithms start with a sort, but that is not what defines them.

Two ₦1,000 notes leave ₦680; a ₦500 leaves ₦180; then ₦100, ₦50, ₦20 and ₦10 finish it: seven pieces. There is no ₦30 note, greedy never skips the ₦10 to take two ₦5s, and it takes the ₦500 before any ₦200.

 Why does largest-note-first always give the fewest pieces for naira notes and coins?

This is an exchange argument. For the naira, two ₦500s become one ₦1,000, five ₦200s become one ₦1,000, and so on. Systems where greedy is always optimal are called canonical; many sets are not.

 The till holds one ₦500 note and three ₦200 notes, nothing else. A customer needs ₦600. What does largest-note-first do?

With a limited supply the swap argument breaks, because you cannot swap for notes you do not have. Greedy commits to the ₦500 and never reconsiders, even though three ₦200 notes would work.

Greedy commits to ₦750 and cannot recover. Treat "greedy found nothing" as proof of nothing unless you have proved the greedy rule correct for that setting.

 The lesson finds the fewest vouchers with a BFS over amounts. What are the vertices and edges of that graph?

BFS finds the shortest path in an unweighted graph, and each step here is one voucher. It is always correct, at a cost of O(amount × number of denominations).

 To accept the most bookings in one meeting room, why is "accept the booking that starts earliest" a bad rule?

Starting early says nothing about how much of the day a booking uses up. One counterexample is enough to reject a greedy rule.

 Why is "accept the shortest booking first" wrong?

Shortest first often does well, which is what makes it dangerous. The brute-force test in the lesson found a counterexample after 19 random days.

 Why is "accept the booking that ends earliest" the right greedy rule for one room?

That swap is the exchange argument: the earliest-ending booking ends no later than the best schedule's first booking, so it cannot create a new conflict. Repeat on what is left.

 What is the idea of an exchange argument?

For one meeting room: swap the optimal schedule's first booking for greedy's earliest-ending booking; the schedule stays valid and the same size. Repeat for each later booking.

 "Greedy stays ahead" for the one-room problem means:

If greedy is never behind after each step, another schedule cannot fit a booking that greedy could not also fit, so it cannot end up with more bookings.

Brute force is slow but obviously correct, and small inputs keep it fast. Disagreements give you a concrete counterexample. Property-testing libraries such as fast-check automate this and shrink failing inputs.

 To minimise the worst lateness of a rider's deliveries (done one after another), which greedy rule is optimal?

Swapping two neighbouring deliveries that are out of deadline order never makes the worst lateness larger, so repeatedly swapping reaches deadline order without getting worse. Riding times do not enter the rule.

 Dijkstra's algorithm is greedy: it always finalises the closest unfinished city. What makes that choice safe?

The safety of a greedy choice always rests on a property of the problem. For Dijkstra it is non-negative weights; with a negative "rebate" edge the same greedy choice can be wrong.

Checks in application code race with each other. A database constraint is checked atomically when the row is written, no matter which code path writes it.

Many routing and packing problems have no known fast exact algorithm. Fast heuristics that are usually within a few percent of optimal are standard, as long as the objective and the trade-off are explicit.
