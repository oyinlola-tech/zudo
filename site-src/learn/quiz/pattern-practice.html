A cold room logs its temperature every minute. Food safety wants the longest stretch of consecutive minutes during which the temperature stayed below 5 °C, allowing at most two readings above it (sensor glitches). Which pattern fits?

"Longest stretch of consecutive" is a contiguous range of variable length. The window's summary is the number of readings above 5 °C; shrink from the left while it exceeds 2. It is the on-time streak problem with a different story.

 Two carts must be compared: do they contain the same products in the same quantities, in any order? Which pattern fits best?

"Same items, any order" depends only on counts. One table, O(n + m) time. Sorting both carts would also work, in O(n log n).

 Product prices are sorted ascending. Count the pairs of different products whose total fits a ₦20,000 gift card. Which pattern gives O(n)?

Sorted data and a question about pairs. When prices[left] + prices[right] fits, all right − left partners of left fit at once; otherwise right is too expensive for anyone and moves in.

 An e-bike route has charging stops at known distances. What is the smallest battery range that completes the route charging at most 4 times? Which pattern fits?

"The smallest value such that…" with a monotonic check: a bigger battery never needs more charges. It is the truck-capacity search in another costume.

 A dashboard lets thousands of managers ask for total sales between any two dates of the past year. The daily figures do not change. What should you build?

Arbitrary, unrelated ranges over static data are exactly what prefix sums are for: O(n) once, O(1) per query.

 Net cash flow per day can be negative. You need the number of periods whose total is exactly ₦0. Why not a shrinking sliding window?

A range from i to j totals 0 exactly when the running totals before i and at j are equal. Counting equal running totals is a frequency count.

 A reservoir's level only ever falls during a drought. Given the daily levels, find the first day it drops below the safe level. Which approach is fastest?

A monotonic yes/no over positions: the first true is the answer, in O(log n). Sorting would destroy the day order you need.

 Transactions arrive one by one from a webhook. You must report two that sum to a refund as soon as both have arrived. Why is a Map better than two pointers here?

The pattern follows the constraints: complete and sorted data suits two pointers; streaming, unsorted data suits a lookup table.

 A nightly job compares the 25 items of two small lists with a nested loop. A reviewer asks you to replace it with a frequency counter. What is the best response?

Patterns earn their complexity on large inputs or hot paths. Asking "how big is the input, and how often does this run?" comes before choosing a pattern.

 A radio station must split a day's ads (in order, with durations) into 6 consecutive blocks, making the longest block as short as possible. Which earlier problem is this?

"Split into k consecutive groups, minimise the largest" is always the same search: check a limit greedily in O(n), and binary search the limit between the longest ad and the total.

 For "the shortest span of events that includes every warehouse", what summary does the sliding window keep?

The counts let you update when an event leaves; the "covered" number tells you in O(1) whether the window is valid, without checking every warehouse each step.

 In the shortest-cover window, when does left move?

For "shortest valid", shrink while valid. For "longest valid" (like the on-time streak), shrink only while invalid.

 Managers try hundreds of starting-stock values against the same 90-day sales plan. Why prefix sums plus binary search, instead of scanning the plan for each value?

Precompute once, query many times. The precondition (no negative sales) is what makes the running totals monotonic.

 For the longest balanced card/cash run, the Map stores the first position of each running balance and never updates it. Why?

For counting ranges you need how many times each balance appeared; for the longest range you need where it first appeared. Same table idea, different question.

 Traffic data is not sorted, yet binary search finds a peak minute. What justifies discarding half?

Binary search needs a proof that the answer is on one side, not sorted data. Here the proof comes from comparing neighbours.

 The "two orders within 10 minutes" check keeps only each customer's most recent order. What must be true of the input?

Sort or buffer late events first, or compare against every order in the last 10 minutes. The logic was right; the precondition was not.

 In a coding interview you are given a problem statement. What should you do first?

The constraints decide the pattern: "sorted?" chooses two pointers over a Map; "streaming?" rules out two pointers; "negative values?" rules out a shrinking window.

 You solved pair-sum with a Map. The interviewer adds: "the list is sorted and you may not allocate extra memory". What now?

Changing plan when constraints change, and saying why the new approach is correct, is exactly what the follow-up is testing.

 Any peak is a correct answer, so the fast and brute-force versions may return different peaks. How does the harness still test findPeak?

When several answers are valid, test the property every valid answer has. Comparing with one reference answer would fail correct code.

Keeping generated inputs small (a dozen items) means the first failure is usually small enough to trace. The lesson also runs a deliberately broken version, so you know the harness can fail.
