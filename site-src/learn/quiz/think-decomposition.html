What is decomposition?

Nobody can write "a banking app" in one go. Decomposition splits it into users, accounts, transfers and so on, then splits each of those, until every piece is one clear step.

 When is a piece small enough to stop splitting?

A leaf is one clear step: here, a single comparison with a yes or no answer. "Make it secure" and "Handle the money" are goals, not steps; each still needs splitting.

 What is top-down design?

Top-down starts at the top of the tree ("banking application") and works down to the leaves. It is the best way to understand a problem and make sure nothing is forgotten.

 Which of these is an example of working bottom-up?

Bottom-up starts from small pieces you know you need, makes them work, and combines them into bigger features. The fee piece was tested at its boundaries first, then used by the transfer check.

 What does "design top-down, build bottom-up" mean in practice?

Top-down finds all the pieces and how they connect. Bottom-up building means every piece you combine already works. Real teams use both.

 What is the main risk of working only top-down?

Top-down is good at not forgetting pieces, but if you only ever plan, nothing runs until the very end, and problems in the plan show up late. Building leaves early catches them sooner.

 What is the main risk of working only bottom-up?

Without a top-down picture, you can build a beautiful fee calculator in the wrong currency, or a notifications piece that expects data no other piece provides.

 What does "Transfers depends on Accounts" mean?

A transfer moves money between accounts. Without the Accounts piece there is nothing to move money between, so Accounts is built first.

 A shop system has: Products (needs nothing), Customers (needs nothing), Cart (needs Products, Customers), Orders (needs Cart), Receipts (needs Orders). Which build order works?

Each piece must come after everything it needs. Cart needs both Products and Customers, so both must come before it. The last option builds Cart before Customers.

 In the bank, Authentication needs only Users, and Accounts needs only Users. Once Users is built, what does that tell you?

Neither needs the other, only Users. So once Users exists, they can be built in parallel, by different people, in either order.

 Your plan says piece A needs piece B, and piece B needs piece A. What does that usually mean?

With a circle, neither piece can be built or tested first. Moving the shared part into its own piece, which both can use, removes the circle.

 The notifications piece only needs a phone number and a message; it knows nothing about transfers. Why is that good?

The fewer things a piece needs from others, the safer it is to change. A piece with small, clear needs can be replaced without anything else noticing.

 A transfer subtracts ₦15,000 from account A, then the server crashes before adding it to account B. What is the problem, and what does the plan need?

"Move the money" is two leaves that are not independent. The plan must say they succeed or fail together. Databases provide this with a transaction, which you will use later.

 A slow network makes a member tap "Send ₦15,000" twice, and both requests arrive. Which missing leaf would stop ₦30,000 from moving?

Both requests pass every normal check. Giving each transfer request a unique reference, and refusing a second request with the same reference, is the leaf the first tree forgot.

 The money moved correctly, but the SMS company is down. What should happen to the transfer?

Moving the money is essential; the SMS is "best effort". Notifications are a separate piece so that their failure does not undo a correct transfer and can be retried.

 Why does the transfer check "does this person own account A?" before "is A's balance big enough?"

With else if, only the first failed check is reported. If the balance came first, a stranger trying to send from Ada's account would get "not enough money" or not, which leaks information about her balance.

 The withdrawal piece checks amount <= balance. The transfer piece has its own copy that checks amount < balance. What is the best fix?

The bug exists because the same rule lives in two places and the copies drifted apart. One shared piece means one rule, fixed and tested once.

 Why is it better to split an app by job (check, move, record, tell) than by screen (home page, send money page)?

"You can only send from your own account" is a rule of the job, not of one page. Split by job, and every screen becomes a thin layer on top of the same, tested pieces.

 Which of these is an integration test rather than a unit test?

A unit test checks one piece alone. "A refused transfer leaves both balances unchanged" checks that the check piece and the move piece work correctly together: that is a test of the join.

 In large systems, where do most serious failures happen?

Small pieces are easy to test completely. The trouble comes where pieces meet: one succeeds and the next fails, or one sends data the other does not expect. Plan the arrows as carefully as the boxes.
