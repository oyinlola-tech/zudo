Why can't ShopFlow wrap "charge, reserve, book courier" in one BEGIN … COMMIT any more?

A transaction is a promise made by one database. With four databases (and a payment provider's HTTP API), no single system can promise all-or-nothing across them.

 In two-phase commit, what does a participant promise when it votes "yes" in the prepare phase?

After voting yes, the participant can no longer decide on its own. That is what makes 2PC atomic, and what makes a coordinator failure so painful.

 The coordinator crashes after every participant voted yes but before it sent "commit". What happens?

A prepared participant cannot know whether the others were told to commit or abort, so neither choice is safe. Rows stay locked and other customers wait.

 Four services are each available 99.9% of the time. Roughly how often are all four available together, as a 2PC transaction needs?

0.999⁴ ≈ 0.996. A protocol that needs every participant at once multiplies their unavailability: about 35 hours a year instead of 9.

 Why can't a payment provider's REST API take part in 2PC with ShopFlow's databases?

A participant must implement prepare, commit and abort. HTTP APIs, message brokers and e-mail services generally do not, which is one of the main reasons sagas exist.

 What is a saga?

Each step commits immediately in one service's database. Consistency comes from compensations, not from rollback.

 The saga must undo a captured ₦25,000 payment. Which compensation is right?

A compensation is a new business action that semantically undoes the old one. The charge happened, the customer's bank shows it, and the refund is another fact next to it.

 Steps "charge", "reserve" and "book courier" succeeded; "confirm order" then fails permanently. In which order do the compensations run?

Compensations run in reverse order, like unwinding a stack, so each one finds the world as its step left it.

 A saga step sends the customer a "your order is confirmed" SMS. Later, a step fails. What is the compensation?

Some steps cannot be undone. Put them last, after the steps that can fail, or accept a compensating message instead of an undo.

 What is the pivot of a saga?

Before the pivot, failures compensate. After it, failures are retried until the saga completes. Irreversible steps belong at or after the pivot.

 20% of orders fail because the item is out of stock. Why is "reserve, then charge" better than "charge, then reserve"?

Put steps that fail often and are cheap to undo first, and expensive or irreversible ones last. The practice section measured 18 refunds against 0.

 While a checkout saga runs, another request reads the order. What should it see, and how is that handled?

Sagas have no isolation: each step is committed and visible. Design for it: explicit pending states, and code that treats them as "in progress, do not touch".

 The charge step times out. The saga does not know whether the card was charged. What should it do?

An unknown outcome is resolved by asking again safely. With the same key there is exactly one charge either way. A new key could charge twice.

 Why must every saga step and compensation be idempotent?

The step's effect and the orchestrator's progress live in different databases and cannot be saved atomically. So "at least once" is the best guarantee, and idempotency turns it into "effectively once".

 A refund compensation returns 503. What should the orchestrator do?

A saga that stops compensating halfway leaves the customer charged without an order. Compensations have no "give up" branch, so design them so they can always eventually succeed.

 Which statement about orchestration and choreography is right?

An orchestrator can be read top to bottom and answers "where is order 42?" with one row. Choreography suits short reactions where services should not know about each other.

 What is a real cost of choreography?

No service knows the whole flow. That keeps them decoupled, and also makes the flow hard to see, change and debug, and makes accidental cycles easy.

 A service saves an order, then publishes order.placed to the broker. What can go wrong?

That is a dual write: two systems, no shared transaction, a crash window in between. Reordering the calls just moves the bug (an event for an order that was never saved).

 What makes the transactional outbox reliable?

"Send a message" becomes "insert a row", which the database's transaction protects. The relay retries publishing until the row is marked published.

 The relay publishes an outbox row, then crashes before marking it published. What happens next?

That is why the outbox gives at-least-once delivery. Duplicates are handled on the consumer side, with an inbox or idempotent operations.

 Two relay instances claim outbox rows with … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED. What does SKIP LOCKED do?

Without it, the second relay would wait for the first relay's lock, then publish the same rows. With it, work is shared out. (PGlite is a single connection, so the lesson could show the query but not two relays competing.)

 Why must the inbox insert be in the same transaction as the charge it protects?

Recording "done" and doing it must be atomic. Recorded without the effect, the message is lost; the effect without the record, it is repeated.

 A relay gives each send a fresh random message id. Why does that break the consumer's inbox?

The id must identify the message, not the delivery. Deriving it from the outbox row (or the event's own id) keeps it the same on every attempt.

 Why does the orchestrator save the saga's step and status in the orders database after every step?

An orchestrator that keeps progress only in memory forgets every saga in flight when it dies: money charged, no order, and nobody knows.

 The orders service starts the saga in a @zudojs/transactions after-commit callback. If the saga throws there, what happens?

After-commit work cannot undo the commit. That is why the saga row is written in the transaction: the database, not the callback, is the source of truth.

 The lesson's relay claims rows by setting claimed_until to a short time in the future. Why a lease instead of a permanent "claimed" flag?

Any claim made by a process that can die needs an expiry. The same idea protects sagas from two orchestrators working on them at once.
