How is a sequence different from a set?

A sequence is an ordered list of terms: position 3 is a specific term, and the same value may appear twice. A set only cares about membership.

 The terms 7,000, 7,250, 7,500, 7,750 have differences of 250 each time. What kind of sequence is it?

Constant differences mean each step adds the same amount: arithmetic, with common difference 250.

 A shop's orders go 400, 600, 900, 1,350. What is the rule?

The differences (200, 300, 450) change, but the ratios are all 1.5: a geometric sequence growing 50% per step.

 You see the first four terms 2, 4, 8, 16 in some data. What can you conclude?

A few terms suggest a rule; they do not prove it. Many different rules produce the same first terms. Use patterns to check a rule you got from the source.

 Rent starts at ₦400,000 (year 0) and rises ₦25,000 a year. Which expression gives the rent in year n?

An arithmetic sequence adds the common difference once per step: an = a0 + n × d.

 A van worth ₦10,000,000 loses 20% of its value each year. Which expression gives its value after n years?

Losing 20% means keeping 80%, so each year multiplies by 0.8: geometric decay. Subtracting ₦2,000,000 a year would be linear and reach zero after 5 years, which the real value never does.

 Instalments are ₦10,000, ₦12,000, ₦14,000, …, ₦28,000 (10 payments). What is their total?

Sum = number of terms × (first + last) / 2 = 10 × (10,000 + 28,000) / 2 = 190,000. Forgetting to halve gives ₦380,000; ₦280,000 is ten times the last payment.

 Plan A adds ₦50,000 a month; Plan B grows 5% a month from the same start. What happens in the long run?

Linear growth adds the same amount; exponential growth adds the same percentage of an ever larger number. However the two start, exponential growth eventually wins.

 What makes interest "compound"?

Compound interest makes the balance a geometric sequence: P × (1 + r)n. Simple interest pays the same amount every period, an arithmetic sequence.

 After exactly one period, how do simple and compound interest compare?

In the first period there is no earlier interest to earn interest on, so both add P × r. The difference starts from the second period.

 A loan charges 15% per month, compounded. Roughly what is the effective yearly cost?

1.1512 is about 5.35, so a year multiplies the debt by 5.35: an effective rate of about 435%. Multiplying 15% by 12 (180%) ignores compounding.

 Why is 1% interest per month worth slightly more than 12% once a year?

1.0112 ≈ 1.1268, so the effective annual rate is about 12.68%. More frequent compounding means interest starts earning sooner.

 What is a recurrence relation?

"balance(n) = balance(n − 1) × 1.01 + 20,000" is a recurrence. A loop computes it naturally: the variable holds the current term, and each round applies the rule.

 A saver deposits at the start of each month, before interest. Which update is right?

A start-of-month deposit is in the account when interest is calculated, so it earns that month's interest. The first option is the end-of-month version. The account terms decide which is correct.

 ₦200,000 is borrowed at 4% a month, with repayments of ₦7,000 a month. What happens?

A repayment must be larger than the interest charged, or the debt grows every month. A loop waiting for it to reach zero would never end.

 Why does the repayment schedule cap the loop at 600 months even after checking the payment?

The first check handles the known hopeless case. The cap handles anything you did not think of. Every while loop that waits for a condition should have a bound.

 Which loop runs exactly 12 times?

1 to 12 inclusive is 12 rounds. Starting at 0 with <= 12 gives 13 rounds; 1 to < 12 gives 11.

 How can you test a general loan-schedule function with a case you can check by hand?

With no interest, ₦100,000 at ₦30,000 a month is clearly 4 payments, the last ₦10,000. A general routine must agree with special cases you can solve yourself.

 Why should a loan schedule round the interest to whole kobo each month rather than only at the end?

Each month the ledger shows a real interest charge in kobo. Rounding per step matches that ledger and stops floating-point drift from piling up.

 Using the rule of 72, roughly how long does something growing 8% a year take to double?

72 / 8 = 9. It is an estimate for sanity checks, not a replacement for the exact calculation.

 In a loop computing Fibonacci numbers with previous and current, why compute next before updating either variable?

The recurrence uses the two previous terms. Save the new term, then shift: previous = current; current = next;.
