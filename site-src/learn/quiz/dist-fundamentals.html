What is partial failure?

In one process a call returns or throws. Across machines, payments may have charged the card while its reply was lost, so the caller sees a failure for work that happened. That uncertainty is what distributed designs are built around.

 Orders called payments and timed out after 200 ms. What does orders know?

All of those worlds look the same from the caller: silence. The safe responses are to retry with an idempotency key, or to ask payments later what happened to that key.

 Which delivery guarantee can you build over a network that loses and duplicates messages?

Retries make sure a message gets through, and create duplicates. Remembering processed ids makes the duplicates harmless. "Exactly once" in product documentation usually means exactly this pair.

 Why does the simulator draw its random numbers from a seeded generator instead of Math.random()?

A test that fails once in a hundred runs and cannot be reproduced teaches nothing. With a seed, the failing run is a number you can paste into a bug report. (Seeded generators like mulberry32 are not safe for cryptography; use crypto for secrets.)

 What is the main benefit of running the network in simulated time (a discrete-event simulation)?

The simulator keeps a timeline sorted by simulated time and jumps from event to event. A 5-second outage takes no real time to test, and two runs never disagree because of a slow machine.

 A payments server pauses for 450 ms (a garbage-collection pause) and the failure detector, with a 250 ms timeout, declares it dead. What is this, and why is it dangerous?

A slow node and a dead node send the same thing: nothing. Systems must survive a node that was wrongly declared dead, for example with leases and fencing tokens that make the old owner's late writes fail.

 You lower a failure detector's timeout from 600 ms to 250 ms. What changes?

No timeout is both fast and never wrong. Pick it from measured latencies and pauses, and often require several missed heartbeats before acting.

 Ada's second address change was stamped with an earlier time than her first, and last-write-wins kept the old address. What caused it?

Clock skew between machines is normal. Last-write-wins with wall clocks silently discards whichever write happens to carry the smaller stamp. Use a version from one authority, or a logical clock.

 Which clock should you use to measure how long a request took, or to implement a deadline?

A wall clock can be stepped backwards or forwards by NTP, which makes durations negative and deadlines fire at the wrong time. A monotonic clock counts elapsed time only.

 A service whose Lamport clock is at 4 receives a message stamped 9. What is the time of the receive event?

On receive: take the maximum of your clock and the message's stamp, then count the event: max(4, 9) + 1 = 10. That jump is what makes every effect's time larger than its cause's.

 Event A has Lamport time 3 and event B has Lamport time 7. What can you conclude?

Lamport clocks guarantee one direction only: if A happened before B, then time(A) < time(B). A smaller number does not prove causality. To tell "before" from "concurrent", you need vector clocks.

 What can vector clocks tell you that Lamport clocks cannot?

A vector clock keeps one counter per node. If each clock is ahead in some entry, the events were concurrent, like Ada's two offline cart edits, and must be merged instead of one winning.

 Ada saves a new address, reloads the page, and sees her old address. Which consistency guarantee was violated?

Her read went to a follower that had not received her write yet. A session guarantee (remember the version you wrote, skip replicas that are behind) fixes exactly this, without making every read go to the leader.

 An order page shows "shipped", and on the next refresh shows "paid" again. Which guarantee was violated?

The two refreshes were served by replicas with different lag. Eventual consistency allows this; the monotonic-reads session guarantee forbids it by never accepting a version older than one already seen.

 What does eventual consistency promise?

It is the weakest useful promise: no bound on staleness and no ordering between clients. It is fine for view counters and recommendations, and dangerous for stock and money.

 What does the CAP theorem actually say?

Partitions happen whether you choose them or not, so the real choice is what each operation does while one lasts. Outside partitions, both can hold, although consistency still costs latency (PACELC).

 During a partition, which ShopFlow operation should prefer consistency (and sometimes answer "try again")?

Selling stock that does not exist costs refunds and trust, so a clear error is better. Recommendations, counters and carts can be stale or merged later, so they stay available.

 With 3 replicas, writes wait for 2 confirmations (W = 2) and reads ask 2 replicas (R = 2). Why does every read see the latest confirmed write?

Overlapping quorums guarantee that at least one replica in every read set has the newest write. With one replica down, both reads and writes still find 2 replicas.

 There is no partition. Why is a write that waits for a majority of replicas still a trade-off?

PACELC extends CAP: under a Partition choose Availability or Consistency, Else choose Latency or Consistency. Most of the time you are paying the second price, not the first.

 A payments instance crashed, but for several seconds the registry still lists it and clients keep calling it. Why?

Discovery learns about crashes the same way a failure detector does: by silence, after a delay. Shorter TTLs shrink the window; timeouts and retries on another instance cover what remains.

 In server-side service discovery, who picks the instance that handles a request?

Kubernetes Services, DNS names behind a load balancer and service meshes work this way, so application code rarely talks to a registry. Client-side discovery puts the lookup and the choice in the caller instead.

 A seed sweep reports "charged twice in 237 of 500 runs, first failing seed 1". What is the most useful thing about that report?

The percentage depends on the simulated fault rates, not on production. The seed is the valuable part: a rare, timing-dependent bug turned into a repeatable test.
